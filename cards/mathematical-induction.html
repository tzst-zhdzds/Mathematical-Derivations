Mathematical Induction

Suppose we want to prove a statement about every natural number. Write P(n) for the statement about n. If we prove P(0) and show that the statement stays true whenever we pass from a number to its successor, why does that cover all of ℕ?

The Principle of Mathematical Induction

To prove that P(n) holds for every n ∈ ℕ, it suffices to prove two things:

  1. Base case. Prove P(0).
  2. Inductive step. Take an arbitrary n ∈ ℕ. Assume P(n) and prove P(S(n)).

The assumption P(n) in the second step is called the induction hypothesis. The goal of that step is to prove the implication P(n) ⇒ P(S(n)) for every n ∈ ℕ.

Why Does Induction Work?

Proof. Suppose we have proved the base case and the inductive step. Collect all the natural numbers for which the statement is true into a set A:

A = {n ∈ ℕ | P(n) holds}.

Our goal is to show that A = ℕ. We already know A ⊆ ℕ because we chose the elements of A from ℕ. To prove the other inclusion, check what the two steps tell us about A.

  • The base case gives P(0), so 0 ∈ A.
  • Suppose n ∈ A. By the definition of A, P(n) holds. The inductive step gives P(S(n)). Since S(n) is also a natural number, S(n) ∈ A.

These are exactly the two conditions for A to be an inductive set. We proved in The Set of Natural Numbers that ℕ is the smallest inductive set. Therefore, ℕ ⊆ A.

We now have both inclusions, so A = ℕ. By the definition of A, this means P(n) holds for every natural number n.

Example

For this proof, let P(n) be the following statement:

n = 0 or n = S(k) for some k ∈ ℕ.

Base case. When n = 0, the first part of the statement is true. Thus P(0) holds.

Inductive step. Take any n ∈ ℕ. To prove P(S(n)), we need to show that S(n) is zero or that it is the successor of some natural number. For the second possibility, choose k = n. Then S(k) = S(n).

In this example, the inductive step works for every n directly, so we do not even need to use the induction hypothesis. By induction, P(n) holds for every n ∈ ℕ. In particular, every nonzero natural number is the successor of some natural number.
